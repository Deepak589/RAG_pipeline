Coffee is one of the most popular beverages in the world, brewed from roasted coffee beans, which are the seeds of berries from the Coffea plant. The two most commercially important species are Coffea arabica and Coffea robusta. Arabica accounts for about 60 percent of world production and is prized for its smoother, more complex flavor, while robusta contains nearly twice the caffeine and has a harsher, more bitter taste.

Coffee cultivation began in Ethiopia, where legend says a goat herder named Kaldi noticed his goats becoming energetic after eating coffee berries. From Ethiopia, coffee spread to Yemen in the 15th century, where Sufi monks drank it to stay awake during night prayers. The port city of Mocha became a major coffee trading hub.

The roasting process transforms green coffee beans through the Maillard reaction, developing hundreds of aroma compounds. Light roasts preserve more of the bean's origin characteristics and acidity, while dark roasts produce bolder, smokier flavors. After roasting, coffee is best consumed within a few weeks as the beans gradually lose their aromatic compounds.

Brewing methods vary widely around the world. Espresso forces hot water through finely ground coffee at high pressure, producing a concentrated shot topped with crema. Pour-over methods like the V60 emphasize clarity and delicate flavors. The French press uses full immersion brewing, yielding a heavier body. Cold brew steeps grounds in cold water for 12 to 24 hours, producing a smooth, low-acid concentrate.
